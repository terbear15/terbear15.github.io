HeatScape

Find the shadier way. HeatScape compares walking routes by how many minutes they spend in direct sun, in the downtowns of twenty large U.S. cities.

What it does

Pick a city, a start and a destination, and how many extra minutes a better route is worth. HeatScape returns three routes: the fastest, a balanced one (about a minute extra), and the shadiest within your budget. In winter it looks for the sunniest route instead. Each route shows minutes in direct sun, a street-by-street list, and the best time to leave. Time and date sliders move the shadows live.

Data pipeline

  • Study areas from official boundaries (NYC NTAs, LA County, city neighborhood layers), clipped to land with Census places.
  • Walking network from OpenStreetMap with OSMnx. Each segment is offset to its two sidewalks, so shade is sampled where people walk, not on the centerline.
  • Building heights and tree crowns from USGS 3DEP LiDAR, gridded at 2 m into a canopy height model. Tree crowns are labeled with scipy and polygonized with rasterio.
  • Missing heights predicted by a scikit-learn random forest from footprint shape, OSM tags and neighboring heights, with 5-fold cross-validated error reported per city.
  • City data builds run on GitHub Actions.

Shade and routing

For each 15-minute slot after departure, the sun position sweeps every building and tree crown into a 2 m shade grid. A time-dependent Dijkstra search updates the sun as the walk goes on and chooses the better sidewalk on each segment. Everything runs in the browser in a Web Worker, with no server.

Limits

HeatScape is a model, not a forecast. It shows shadows from mapped buildings and trees under a clear sky. It does not measure temperature, clouds, awnings or safety, and tree data reflect the LiDAR survey year (2014–2022).
